# Writing Correction — Reference

Use this when the input is the learner's **own French writing** (journal entry, essay, message),
not a transcript to clean. This is a different task from Mode 1's transcript breakdown: the goal is
targeted correction and encouragement, not exhaustive grammar teaching of every sentence.

**Bridge language: English.** All explanatory/meta text is in English, consistent with the rest of
this skill.

## Output structure

```markdown
# [Title] · French Correction

## 1. Corrected French (clean version)
[The full corrected text, clean and readable, preserving the learner's original voice/word choices
wherever they were already correct or merely stylistic. Don't rewrite for style — only fix errors.]

## 2. Error table
| Original | Correction | Type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ... | ... | spelling / collocation / grammar / article / verb form / calque from English |

## 3. Grammar points worth explaining (not just spelling)
[For each error type that reflects a genuine grammar gap (not just a typo), explain the rule with
1-2 extension examples. Skip this for pure spelling slips — spend the words on the errors that will
recur if not understood. Flag calque errors (structure copied from English) as their own category —
see Key Principles below.]

## 4. What you got right (keep doing this)
[Explicitly list phrases, idioms, or structures the learner used correctly and well — especially
anything above their nominal level. This section matters: it's how the learner calibrates their
real level (often higher than they think) and what to keep reusing. Don't skip this even if errors
dominate the text.]

## 5. Next steps (optional)
[2-3 small concrete follow-up actions: read the corrected version aloud, reuse a phrase they
already know in the next entry, self-check for their 1-2 most frequent error types before their
next submission.]
```

## Key principles

- **Fix errors, don't rewrite voice.** If the learner's sentence is grammatically fine but you'd
  phrase it differently, leave it. This is correction, not ghostwriting.
- **Distinguish typo-level slips from real gaps.** A missing accent is not the same category of
  problem as a systematic error (e.g. always forgetting the infinitive after `aller`). Flag the
  systematic ones as patterns worth fixing once, not once per occurrence.
- **Watch specifically for English-calque errors**, since English is this learner's bridge language
  and the most likely source of structural interference. Direct-translation calques are usually the
  most instructive corrections because they reveal a structural mismatch, not just a vocabulary gap.
  Example: *"j'ai été refusé pour un visa"* (calque of "I was rejected for a visa") should become
  *"ma demande de visa a été refusée"* — explain **why** French restructures the sentence around a
  different subject (agent/patient roles), not just supply the fix. Other recurring calque patterns
  to watch for: subject/object reversal verbs (*plaire à*, *manquer à*, *falloir*), missing partitive
  articles English doesn't require, and preposition choices that don't map (English "to" often
  becomes *de* rather than *à*, and vice versa).
- **Always find something genuinely good.** Even in a weak draft there is usually at least one
  correctly-used idiom, connector, or structure above the learner's baseline level. Naming it
  explicitly is not just encouragement — it's calibration data the learner needs (their level may be
  higher, in specific pockets, than their errors suggest).
- **Track recurring error types across sessions if you have access to past corrections** (e.g. via
  conversation search/memory). If the same category of mistake (e.g. dropped infinitive, missing
  partitive article, a specific calque pattern) shows up again, say so explicitly — this is more
  useful feedback than treating every entry as a fresh slate.
